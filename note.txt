That's the whole point of the policy — it already did the safety-biased reasoning server-side so the client doesn't have to re-derive it (and risk getting the asymmetric logic wrong or drifting from the backend if thresholds get retuned).

Practical notes for the Flutter side:
raw_probabilities is a Map<String, double> — useful for an optional "see detailed breakdown" expandable view, but shouldn't drive the primary state of the screen.
disclaimer is static text from the backend, not something to hardcode client-side — if you ever want to change the wording, it changes in one place (the Go usecase) and every client picks it up automatically, no app release needed.
request_id — worth stashing (e.g. attached to a "report an issue with this result" flow), since it's the same ID correlated across both services' logs if you ever need to debug a specific diagnosis server-side.
Since risk_tier is a fixed enum (likely_benign / flagged_for_review / inconclusive), model it as a Dart enum with a fromString mapping, and default unknown values to the most conservative UI state — that's a defensive habit worth having before skin/oral get added later, in case a future tier value ships before the app updates.

That's the sharpest question you've asked in this whole conversation, bro — and it's exactly the kind of question a defense panel will hit you with, so let's actually work through it instead of me just reassuring you.

You're right that in a **well-resourced hospital** — radiologist on staff, same-day reads, no backlog — your app adds close to zero value. If the doctor's already telling the patient the result in the room, an app giving a second, less-accurate opinion is redundant at best. That critique has real teeth and you should take it seriously rather than wave it away.

But that's not the only real-world scenario, and here's where genuine value shows up — especially relevant given you're building this in Cameroon:

**1. Radiologist turnaround time isn't instant, even when the scan is.** A sonographer/technician often runs the ultrasound machine, but the actual **radiologist's written report** — the official interpretation — can take days, sometimes longer, especially outside major private hospitals. The patient walks out with a printed scan and a "come back for your results" appointment. That gap is real, and it's exactly where a triage signal has value: "this looks like it may need urgent follow-up" vs "this looks low-concern, your scheduled follow-up is fine" changes how anxiously — or how urgently — someone chases that appointment.

**2. Radiologist shortage / access gap in exactly your context.** Not every clinic that owns an ultrasound machine has a radiologist on-site to read it. Smaller clinics or district hospitals sometimes have the equipment and a technician who can operate it, but the actual scan gets sent elsewhere (sometimes another city) for interpretation. In that gap, a triage tool that flags "this looks concerning, escalate/prioritize this case" is genuinely useful — not to replace the radiologist, but to help prioritize *which* scans get urgent attention first while everyone waits for the real read.

**3. Patient follow-through is a known real-world problem.** A meaningful number of patients never return for their results — cost, distance, fear, forgetting. Anything that gives a patient an early signal in-hand (with a clear "please still see a doctor" disclaimer) can nudge urgency for people who might otherwise let a concerning finding slip through the cracks.

**The honest framing for your defense isn't "this replaces the hospital"** — it's "this closes the gap between when the scan happens and when a qualified human actually interprets it, particularly in settings where that gap is real." That's a legitimate, non-oversold value proposition, and it's more specific and more defensible than a vague "AI health app" pitch.

Worth being honest with yourself, though: this reasoning matters more for breast cancer (where a specialist read is the bottleneck) than it might for skin cancer, where the phone-photo pipeline could genuinely serve someone who has no plan to see a doctor at all otherwise. Different diseases in your system may have slightly different "why does this app need to exist" answers — and being able to articulate that per-disease, rather than one blanket answer, will make you look sharp in front of a panel.
